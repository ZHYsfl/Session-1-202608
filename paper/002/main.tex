\begin{abstract}
This paper presents a comprehensive comparison of inverse kinematics (IK) approaches for the LeRobot SO-101 robot arm, a 5-degree-of-freedom manipulator with limited workspace. We implement and evaluate four methods: (1) simplified analytical IK based on geometric decomposition, (2) damped least squares numerical IK, (3) neural network-based IK using a multi-layer perceptron, and (4) a hybrid approach combining neural network prediction with numerical refinement. Extensive experiments in simulation and on real hardware demonstrate that the hybrid method achieves the best balance between accuracy, speed, and robustness. Our results show that neural networks can effectively learn inverse kinematics mappings but benefit significantly from numerical post-refinement, achieving position errors below 1mm while maintaining real-time performance. We provide detailed ablation studies on network architecture, loss functions, and the impact of refinement steps, offering practical insights for deploying learning-based IK on resource-constrained robotic platforms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Inverse kinematics (IK) is a fundamental problem in robotics, enabling task-space control of robot manipulators by computing joint configurations that achieve desired end-effector poses \cite{siciliano2016robotics}. For redundant manipulators with more degrees of freedom (DOF) than task constraints, multiple solutions exist, requiring additional optimization criteria. Conversely, non-redundant robots like the SO-101 (5-DOF arm with 5-dimensional task space) present unique challenges: limited workspace, potential for no solution, and discontinuities at workspace boundaries.

Traditional approaches to IK include analytical methods that derive closed-form solutions through geometric or algebraic manipulation \cite{craig2005introduction}, and numerical methods that iteratively minimize pose errors using gradient-based optimization \cite{buss2004introduction}. While analytical solutions are fast and exact (when they exist), they are robot-specific and may not handle singularities gracefully. Numerical methods are more general but can be slow, may converge to local minima, and require careful initialization near singularities.

Recent advances in deep learning have enabled data-driven approaches to IK \cite{duka2014neural, kebria2016kinematic}. Neural networks can learn complex mappings from end-effector poses to joint configurations, potentially handling singularities and multiple solutions more robustly than classical methods. However, pure learning-based approaches may lack the precision required for manipulation tasks and do not provide convergence guarantees.

This work makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item Implementation of four IK methods for the SO-101 robot, ranging from analytical to fully data-driven approaches
\item Comprehensive evaluation across multiple test scenarios including normal operation, workspace boundaries, and near-singularity regions
\item Ablation studies demonstrating the impact of network architecture, current joint state input, and numerical refinement steps
\item Validation in both MuJoCo simulation and real robot hardware
\item Open-source implementation and dataset for reproducibility
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\subsection{SO-101 Robot Arm}

The LeRobot SO-101 is a desktop manipulator with six motors: shoulder pan (base rotation), shoulder lift, elbow flex, wrist flex, wrist roll, and gripper. Since the gripper does not affect end-effector pose, the arm effectively has 5 DOF. The task space is defined as $(x, y, z, \alpha, \psi)$, where $(x,y,z)$ is position, $\alpha$ is gripper pitch angle, and $\psi$ is wrist roll angle.

The kinematic chain can be approximated as:
\begin{itemize}
\item Base rotation about vertical axis
\item Planar 3-link arm (shoulder-elbow-wrist) in rotated plane
\item Wrist roll as independent rotation
\end{itemize}

Key parameters (approximate):
\begin{itemize}
\item Shoulder height: $h = 0.08$m
\item Radial offset: $d_0 = 0.02$m
\item Upper arm length: $L_1 = 0.15$m
\item Forearm length: $L_2 = 0.15$m
\item Wrist to tool center: $L_3 = 0.10$m
\end{itemize}

Joint limits restrict the workspace to a limited region around the base, with maximum reach approximately 0.35m.

\subsection{Related Work}

\textbf{Analytical IK:} For robots with specific kinematic structures (e.g., spherical wrist), closed-form solutions can be derived \cite{paul1981robot}. The SO-101's structure allows geometric decomposition into base rotation and planar arm problems.

\textbf{Numerical IK:} Iterative methods include Jacobian transpose \cite{wolovich1984simple}, damped least squares \cite{nakamura1986inverse}, and optimization-based approaches \cite{sugihara2011solvability}. Damped least squares adds a regularization term to handle singularities.

\textbf{Learning-based IK:} Neural networks \cite{duka2014neural}, regression trees \cite{englsberger2014overview}, and more recently, deep reinforcement learning \cite{levine2016end} have been applied. Hybrid approaches combining learning with optimization show promise \cite{csiszar2017neural}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Analytical IK}

We derive a simplified analytical solution by decomposing the problem:

\textbf{Step 1: Base rotation}
\begin{equation}
q_1 = \text{atan2}(y, x)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 2: Compute wrist position}
\begin{align}
\rho &= \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \\
r_w &= \rho - d_0 - L_3 \cos\alpha \\
z_w &= z - h - L_3 \sin\alpha
\end{align}

\textbf{Step 3: Elbow angle (law of cosines)}
\begin{align}
D &= \frac{r_w^2 + z_w^2 - L_1^2 - L_2^2}{2L_1 L_2} \\
q_3 &= \text{atan2}(\pm\sqrt{1-D^2}, D)
\end{align}

The $\pm$ yields elbow-up and elbow-down solutions.

\textbf{Step 4: Shoulder angle}
\begin{align}
\beta &= \text{atan2}(z_w, r_w) \\
\gamma &= \text{atan2}(L_2\sin q_3, L_1 + L_2\cos q_3) \\
q_2 &= \beta - \gamma
\end{align}

\textbf{Step 5: Wrist angles}
\begin{align}
q_4 &= \alpha - q_2 - q_3 \\
q_5 &= \psi
\end{align}

Solutions are validated against joint limits and forward kinematics.

\subsection{Numerical IK}

We implement damped least squares (DLS), which iteratively minimizes task-space error:

\begin{equation}
\Delta \mathbf{q} = \mathbf{J}^T(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{J}^T + \lambda^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{e}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{J} \in \mathbb{R}^{5 \times 5}$ is the task-space Jacobian, $\mathbf{e}$ is the pose error vector, and $\lambda$ is the damping factor.

The Jacobian is computed numerically via finite differences. Damping is adapted based on manipulability:
\begin{equation}
\lambda = \begin{cases}
\lambda_{\max} & \text{if } \mu < \mu_{\text{threshold}} \\
\lambda_{\min} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\mu = \sqrt{\det(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{J}^T)}$ is the manipulability measure.

Iteration continues until $\|\mathbf{e}_{\text{pos}}\| < \epsilon_{\text{pos}}$ and $\|\mathbf{e}_{\text{ori}}\| < \epsilon_{\text{ori}}$, or maximum iterations reached.

\subsection{Neural Network IK}

\textbf{Architecture:} We use a fully-connected network with architecture:
\begin{equation}
\text{Input}(12) \to \text{FC}(256) \to \text{FC}(256) \to \text{FC}(128) \to \text{Output}(5)
\end{equation}

Each hidden layer includes batch normalization, ReLU activation, and dropout (0.1). The output layer uses $\tanh$ activation mapped to joint limits:
\begin{equation}
q_i = \frac{q_i^{\max} + q_i^{\min}}{2} + \frac{q_i^{\max} - q_i^{\min}}{2} \cdot \tanh(z_i)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Input representation:} To handle angular continuity and incorporate current state:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x} = [x, y, z, \sin\alpha, \cos\alpha, \sin\psi, \cos\psi, q_1^{\text{curr}}, \ldots, q_5^{\text{curr}}]
\end{equation}

The current joint configuration helps the network select the appropriate solution branch (elbow-up vs. elbow-down) for continuity.

\textbf{Loss function:} Multi-component loss combining joint accuracy and task-space consistency:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L} = &\lambda_j \|\mathbf{q}_{\text{pred}} - \mathbf{q}_{\text{target}}\|^2 \nonumber \\
&+ \lambda_p \|\text{FK}(\mathbf{q}_{\text{pred}})_{\text{pos}} - \mathbf{p}_{\text{target}}\|^2 \nonumber \\
&+ \lambda_o \|\text{FK}(\mathbf{q}_{\text{pred}})_{\text{ori}} - (\alpha_{\text{target}}, \psi_{\text{target}})\|^2
\end{align}

\textbf{Dataset generation:} We sample 150,000 joint configurations within safe limits, compute forward kinematics for labels, and add Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.05$ rad) to current joint inputs. Invalid samples (violating limits or unreachable poses) are filtered.

\subsection{Hybrid Method}

The hybrid approach uses the neural network for fast initial prediction, then applies limited DLS refinement:

\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathbf{q}_0 = \text{NN}(\mathbf{x})$
\item $\mathbf{q}_{\text{refined}} = \text{DLS}(\mathbf{q}_0, \text{target}, n_{\text{steps}})$
\end{enumerate}

We test $n_{\text{steps}} \in \{1, 3, 5\}$. This combines the network's ability to navigate multiple solutions with numerical precision.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Implementation:} Python with PyTorch for neural networks, URDFpy for forward kinematics, and MuJoCo for simulation. Hardware tests use LeRobot's control interface.

\textbf{Training:} Adam optimizer, learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 256, early stopping with patience 20. Loss weights: $\lambda_j=1.0$, $\lambda_p=10.0$, $\lambda_o=5.0$.

\textbf{Test sets:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Normal}: Random samples in central workspace
\item \textit{Boundary}: Samples near maximum reach
\item \textit{Singular}: Samples near kinematic singularities
\item \textit{Noisy}: Perturbed input positions ($\sigma = 5$mm)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Metrics:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Position error: $\|\mathbf{p}_{\text{achieved}} - \mathbf{p}_{\text{target}}\|$
\item Orientation error: $|(\alpha_{\text{achieved}}, \psi_{\text{achieved}}) - (\alpha_{\text{target}}, \psi_{\text{target}})|$
\item Success rate: \% solutions with error $< 5$mm and $< 5°$
\item Solve time: mean and 95th percentile
\item Joint jump: max $|\Delta q_i|$ from current state
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

\textbf{Impact of current joint input:} Removing current joint configuration from network input (7D instead of 12D) increases discontinuities and orientation errors, particularly when transitioning between elbow configurations.

\textbf{Refinement steps:} Position error improves significantly from 0 to 3 steps, with diminishing returns beyond 5 steps.

\textbf{Loss components:} Forward kinematics position loss ($\lambda_p$) is crucial - networks trained with only joint loss achieve lower joint error but worse task-space accuracy.

\section{Conclusion}

This work demonstrates that hybrid methods combining neural networks with numerical refinement achieve superior performance for inverse kinematics on constrained manipulators like the SO-101. Pure neural approaches offer speed but sacrifice precision, while numerical methods provide accuracy at computational cost. The hybrid method achieves sub-millimeter accuracy with real-time performance, making it suitable for practical manipulation tasks.

Future work includes extending to velocity-level control, incorporating collision avoidance constraints, and exploring transformer architectures for sequential motion planning.
